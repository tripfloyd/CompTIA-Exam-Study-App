There's a common mix-up here worth clearing up: Public/private on GitHub is about who can see it — not who can edit it. Making a repo public just means anyone can view the code, download it, or clone it. It does not give anyone permission to change anything in your actual repo. Only people you explicitly add as collaborators (Settings → Collaborators) can push changes directly. Random visitors have zero write access, period. If someone really wants to suggest a change, the only path is: They make their own personal copy ("fork") of your repo They edit their copy They submit a "pull request" asking you to merge their changes into yours And even then, nothing happens to your repo unless you personally review and approve it. You could ignore every pull request forever and your repo would never change. So the advice to keep it private was likely about general caution (not exposing code/business ideas, avoiding drive-by clutter, etc.) — reasonable in general, but for a study tool like this with no sensitive info, going public is safe specifically because "public" ≠ "editable by others." tl;dr: flip it to public, nobody can touch your actual files without your explicit permission, and GitHub Pages becomes free.
